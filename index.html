How this model works (each day)
  1. Development: DTT = mean temperature minus base temperature (maize 8 °C, rice 10 °C, cassava 13 °C), with no extra credit above an optimum temperature and zero at a high cut-off. Stages begin when thermal time reaches set totals.
  2. Light: PAR = 0.5 × solar radiation. Leaves intercept PAR × (1 − e−k × LAI), where k is the light extinction coefficient (Beer's law).
  3. Growth: new biomass = Radiation Use Efficiency (RUE) × intercepted PAR × stress factor.
  4. Water: reference evapotranspiration (ETo) from the Hargreaves equation; crop water use = crop coefficient (Kc) × ETo × water stress coefficient (Ks). Rain fills the soil "bucket"; extra water drains away.
  5. Stress: Ks falls below 1 when the soil has lost more than 55 percent of its available water. Heat above 35 °C also slows growth.
  6. Yield: final biomass × Harvest Index (HI). Water or heat stress during flowering lowers the HI.

What is a crop model?

A crop model is a representation of a crop through mathematical equations that explain how the crop interacts with its environment, both above ground (air temperature, sunlight, day length, rain) and below ground (soil water, nutrients).

InputsWeather, soil, crop variety, management
→
ModelEquations for growth and development
→
OutputsDates of flowering and maturity, dry matter, yield

Give a crop model a season of weather and it tells you when the crop will flower and mature, and roughly how much it will yield. The model at the top of this page is a small working example.

Growth versus development

Growth

The increase in dry matter (the weight of the plant once water is removed). It depends on how fast leaves intercept sunlight and how fast roots capture water and nutrients.

Question it answers: How big and heavy is the crop?

Development

The progress through life stages, called phenophases, from sowing to maturity. It is mostly driven by temperature, and for some stages by day length.

Yield in three parts

Yield = Length of Growing Period (LGP) × Mean rate of dry matter accumulation × Harvest Index (HI)

The Harvest Index (HI) is the fraction of total dry matter that we actually harvest (for maize, the grain). Without stress, HI stays fairly steady from year to year for a given variety.

Part 4 of 9

Daily Thermal Time (DTT) calculator

Crops develop faster when it is warmer. Below a base temperature (Tbase) development stops. Daily Thermal Time (DTT), also called growing degree days, measures the useful heat a crop gets each day.

DTT = ((Tmax + Tmin) / 2) − Tbase, and DTT = 0 if the answer is negative

Note: the lecture notes print this formula on two lines, which can look like only Tbase is divided by 2. The standard form is shown above: first average the maximum and minimum temperatures, then subtract the base temperature. DTT is the heat above the base, not the mean daily temperature itself.

Try this: set Tmin to 18 and Tmax to 26 (a cool highland night like Kwahu) and see how many more days the crop needs than at 32 and 22 in Tamale.

Day length (photoperiod) and Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR)

Day length (photoperiod)

Some stages, especially flowering, respond to how long the day is. Short day crops such as many sorghum, rice and soybean varieties flower sooner when days get shorter.

Day length:

Cape Coast is near 5.1° N, so day length only changes by about half an hour across the year. Tamale is near 9.4° N. Move the latitude to 50° to see how much bigger the change is in Europe.

Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR)

Only part of sunlight drives photosynthesis: light between 400 and 700 nanometres. This is PAR, and it is roughly half (about 45 to 50 percent) of total solar radiation.

Part 6 of 9

The five basic information requirements

To build a crop weather model we must first collect:

  1. Crop phenology in relation to temperature and day length

    When does the crop emerge, flower and mature, and how do temperature and day length change those dates? (The lecture spells this "phonology"; the correct term is phenology.)

  2. Water use by the crop during different phenophases

    How much water the crop uses at each stage. Maize needs the most water around tasselling and silking.

  3. Relationship between radiation interception, crop water use and total dry matter production

    How intercepted sunlight and water use turn into plant weight.

  4. Partitioning of dry matter into plant parts, as affected by water and nutrients

    How the dry matter is shared between roots, stems, leaves and grain. Stress usually lowers the share going to grain (the harvest index).

  5. Effect of weather on biotic interference to crop growth

    How weather favours pests, diseases and weeds. For example, warm humid weather can favour fungal diseases, and dry spells have been linked to fall armyworm outbreaks.

Memory aid: P W R P B, "Plants Want Rain, Plus Balance": Phenology, Water use, Radiation, Partitioning, Biotic.

Sources and limits

This site was made for an Agricultural Meteorology and Climatology group presentation (crop weather modeling) at the University of Cape Coast. The simulator is a teaching demonstration, kept simple on purpose. It ignores nutrients, pests, weeds and many details that real models include, so its yields are closer to what good management could reach than to average farm yields.

Weather data

Daily maximum and minimum temperature, rainfall (corrected precipitation) and solar radiation for 2020 to 2024 come from the NASA Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources (POWER) project, Agroclimatology community, for points near Cape Coast (5.10° N, 1.25° W) and Tamale (9.40° N, 0.85° W). POWER data are satellite and model based on a grid of about 50 kilometres, so they can differ from Ghana Meteorological Agency (GMet) station records. For example, the coastal grid cell for Cape Coast shows more rain than the Cape Coast station usually records. Scenarios change the real data in simple ways (see the scenario list).
