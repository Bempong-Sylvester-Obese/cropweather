Daily Thermal Time (DTT) calculator

DTT = ((maximum temperature + minimum temperature) / 2) − base temperature, and 0 if negative

The lecture prints this formula over two lines; the standard form averages the two temperatures first, then subtracts the base. Maize base temperature is typically about 10 °C.

How the model works, in five steps
  1. Development: each day adds Daily Thermal Time (DTT) = mean temperature minus a base of 10 °C, with no extra benefit above 34 °C. Stages start at set totals: emergence 70, end of juvenile stage 270, flowering 980, grain filling 1120, maturity 1790 °C days.
  2. Light: leaves catch Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR), about half of sunlight. More leaf area, measured as Leaf Area Index (LAI), catches more.
  3. Growth: new dry matter = Radiation Use Efficiency (RUE) × light caught × stress factor.
  4. Water: rain fills the soil; the crop uses water at a rate set by the weather (Hargreaves reference evapotranspiration) and its crop coefficient. When the soil loses more than 55 percent of its available water, growth slows and leaves roll.
  5. Yield: dry matter × Harvest Index (HI). Drought or heat above 35 °C at flowering lowers the Harvest Index (HI).
Glossary
Crop model
Mathematical equations that describe how a crop interacts with the weather above ground and the soil below ground.
Growth
Increase in dry matter (plant weight with the water removed).
Development
Progress through life stages (phenophases), such as emergence, flowering and maturity.
Daily Thermal Time (DTT)
((maximum temperature + minimum temperature) / 2) minus the base temperature, or 0 if negative. Measured in degree Celsius days.
Base temperature
Temperature below which development stops. Typical for maize: 8 to 10 °C; this page uses 10 °C.
Leaf Area Index (LAI)
Leaf area over each square metre of ground.
Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR)
The part of sunlight (400 to 700 nanometres) plants use for photosynthesis, about half of the total.
Harvest Index (HI)
Share of total dry matter that ends up as grain. About 0.48 for maize without stress.
Yield
Length of growing period × mean rate of dry matter accumulation × Harvest Index (HI).
About and sources

Made for a group presentation on crop weather modeling. The simulator is a simplified teaching model built on ideas from real crop models.

  • Weather: NASA Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources (POWER) daily data for 2021, points near Tamale (9.40° N, 0.85° W) and Cape Coast (5.10° N, 1.25° W). Satellite and model based, so it can differ from Ghana Meteorological Agency (GMet) stations; the Cape Coast grid cell is wetter than the town usually is.
  • Thermal time: Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT) CERES-Maize, optimum 34 °C; this demo uses a maize base of 10 °C to match the course handout, while CERES-Maize itself uses 8 °C (Kiniry and Bonhomme, 1991; DSSAT maize chapter, 2011, doi:10.1002/9780891183860.ch9).
  • Mathematics section: Ritchie and NeSmith (1991), Temperature and crop development, in Modeling Plant and Soil Systems, Agronomy Monograph 31 (sine daily temperature cycle used in CERES-Maize); McMaster and Wilhelm (1997), Agricultural and Forest Meteorology 87, 291 to 300 (capped degree days); Sinclair and Muchow (1999), Advances in Agronomy 65, 215 to 265 (Radiation Use Efficiency); Monsi and Saeki (1953), Japanese Journal of Botany 14, 22 to 52 (Beer's law in canopies).
  • Water use and stress: Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 (Allen and others, 1998).
  • Harvest Index (HI) and stages: FAO AquaCrop reference manual (maize).
  • Radiation Use Efficiency (RUE): Lindquist and others (2005), Agronomy Journal 97; APSIM and WOFOST model documentation.
  • Scenarios edit the real 2021 weather: drought removes drizzle under 3 mm, halves other rain and adds a 30 day rain-free spell over flowering; heat wave holds daily highs at 37 °C or more for 22 days near flowering; the El Niño style year has fewer rainy days, 30 percent less rain, a 2 week dry spell and is about 1 °C warmer, because El Niño years in Ghana tend to bring below-normal rainfall, fewer rainy days and longer dry spells (Owusu and Waylen, 2013; Owusu, Darkey and Boadi, 2019, Natural Resources Forum 43; Ghana Meteorological Agency (GMet) State of the Climate 2024); late planting sows 60 days later, so grain filling runs into the end of the rains.
The mathematics behind it

Each visual uses the bundled NASA POWER data and the location and scenario picked above. Nothing here changes the main demo or its yields.

1. Daily average temperature and the 30 °C cap

Over a day, temperature rises and falls roughly like a sine wave between the minimum and the maximum. A symmetric wave spends as long above its middle as below it, so its 24 hour average is exactly the midpoint, which is why Daily Thermal Time (DTT) uses (Tmax + Tmin) / 2.

T(h)=Tmax+Tmin2+Tmax−Tmin2​cos(2π(h−15)24)

The cap clips each hourly value of the sine curve at 30 °C before subtracting the 10 °C base, then averages the 24 hours. This is one of the two common readings of a capped degree day; the other caps the daily maximum before averaging (McMaster and Wilhelm, 1997). The cap is shown here only; the main demo keeps its own rule (no extra benefit above 34 °C).

2. Differential equations, solved one day at a time

A crop model is a set of rates. Soil water (S, in millimetres) and dry matter (B, in grams per square metre) change each day, and the model adds one day's change at a time (Euler's method, step of one day).

dSdt=rain−evapotranspiration−runoff and drainage

dBdt=RUE×intercepted PAR×stress

St+1=St+Δt×dSdt,Δt=1 day

Simplifications: rain counts as 85 percent effective on days over 5 mm and 50 percent otherwise (the rest is runoff); evapotranspiration is Hargreaves reference evapotranspiration times a crop coefficient and a water stress factor (Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) Paper 56, Allen and others, 1998); water above the root zone's capacity drains away; a deepening root zone adds stored water, shown in the total. Radiation Use Efficiency (RUE) is set to 2.5 g per megajoule (MJ) of intercepted Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR), on the low side of field values of about 3.5 to 3.8 g per MJ intercepted PAR for maize (Sinclair and Muchow, 1999), to allow for tropical field losses that the demo does not model.

3. Fourier series: the yearly temperature cycle

Five years of daily mean temperature repeat roughly once a year, so a constant plus a few sine and cosine waves (a Fourier series) fitted by least squares can describe them. The second harmonic catches a second bump within the year.

4. Beer's law: how much light the leaves catch

Light fades as it passes down through layers of leaves, like light through cloudy water. The fraction caught rises quickly at first, then levels off as leaves start shading each other.

fraction intercepted=1−e−k×LAI

After Monsi and Saeki (1953). The model uses k = 0.65, typical for maize.
